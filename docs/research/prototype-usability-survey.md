# Prototype usability survey — 15 required questions + 5 optional, ~5 minutes

Generated by `create-prototype-usability-form.gs`. Paste that into
<https://script.google.com> and run `createPrototypeUsabilityForm`; this file
is the rationale behind it.

## What it is for, and what it is not

This is **not** the `wishlist-survey.md` instrument. That one asks about a
respondent's *real* Myntra wishlist and feeds Part 2/Part 4. This one asks
about **five minutes spent on the live reconnection prototype** —
`wishlist-reconnection-prototype.vercel.app` — using its fixed demo catalog
and its 30-item seeded wishlist, and it feeds the **usability half** of
Part 6/7: the questions `panel-sizing.md` already found a 300–500-person panel
*can* answer, because they don't need a 30-day cohort —

> Do participants understand why the module appeared? (§4 target: ≥80%)
> Can they recover from an unavailable variant?
> Do the states read correctly?
> Is the Buy/Compare split driven by the button fills? (§4.4)

— plus the two questions this round was actually asked for: **can someone
find what they'd saved**, and **is what they get told good enough to act on**.

It does not replace telemetry. Click-through, which button fires first, and
actual recovery behaviour are all logged by the harness already
(`analytics/events.ts` in the prototype repo) and are the authoritative
signal. This form exists for the thing telemetry cannot see: whether the
person understood *why*, and whether they'd trust it enough to stop looking
elsewhere — comprehension and confidence are self-report by nature.

## Before someone fills this in

They need to have just used the prototype, not be describing it from memory.
The form's own first page carries the task list — no separate moderator
script — because this is meant to run unmoderated, at panel scale, not as a
6-person interview. (**The 6 walkthroughs are a different instrument** —
`interview-guide.md` — and probe the person's *real* wishlist, before this
prototype existed for them to react to.)

**The task list lives only in a form item, not in the top-of-form
description.** `setDescription()` alone scrolls out of view on a phone the
moment the respondent starts answering questions — it can't be scrolled back
to without losing their place. So the numbered steps live in an
`addSectionHeaderItem()` block titled "Before you start," sitting on page 1
directly above the consent checkbox — not in `setDescription()`, which now
carries only the welcome line, the link, and the anonymity note. A section
header doesn't add a page break, so "Before you start" is still page 1, but
it's part of the question flow rather than a banner above it, and it's the
thing a respondent scrolling down to find the consent checkbox has to pass
on the way. (An earlier draft had the steps in both places at once — the
description and the section header — so page 1 showed the same four steps
twice; fixed.)

Suggested search terms are given rather than left open, because the demo
catalog is small and specific: `shirt`, `jeans`, `kurta`, `handbag` are
documented in the prototype's own README to resolve to saved items and
produce a complete look (men's shirt → jeans → belt → watch; women's kurta →
handbag → flats → earrings → belt). An unguided respondent typing an
unrelated term would see nothing and rate a working feature as broken.

**The live link is repeated four times in the form itself** — the top
description, the "Before you start" section header, the consent question's
help text, and the post-submit confirmation message — because Google Forms
renders description and help text as unstyled paragraphs, and a description
scrolls out of view on a phone once the questions start. All four point at
the same `PROTOTYPE_URL` constant in the `.gs` file, so updating the
deployment address only means editing it in one place.

## What this prototype offers — and the blind-testing cost of adding it

A second section header, "What this prototype offers," sits on page 1 right
after "Before you start": a plain-language feature list (search-reconnection,
the confidence/fit signal, recovery from an unavailable size, comparison,
pairing, the tap-to-buy decision point), sourced from `~/MVP_OPUS/README.md`
with the internal names (DC-01, E12, gate labels) stripped out.

**This is a deliberate reversal of the blind-testing pass done earlier in
this document.** Telling a respondent what to look for before they use the
prototype means Q3 (comprehension), Q5 (confidence), Q7 ("cleared up what I
was unsure about"), Q12 (the tap-to-buy decision point) and Q15 (pairing) no
longer measure whether the respondent worked this out on their own — they
can now measure it because the form told them. Stated once, here, rather
than re-qualified on every affected question below: read those five
questions' results as **confirmation of a briefed expectation**, not
independent discovery. Q8 exists specifically to recover some of this —
see "The questions" below. If a genuinely blind read is needed later, the
"Before you start" and "What this prototype offers" blocks are the two
things to remove or move behind a debrief page — nothing else in the form
depends on them.

**Feature coverage.** Every bullet in "What this prototype offers" has a
question that tests it, so the reveal isn't just marketing copy sitting
next to an instrument that never checks it: search-reconnection → Q2; the
confidence/fit signal → Q5, Q7, Q9; recovery from an unavailable size → Q11;
comparison → Q14; pairing → Q15; the tap-to-buy decision point → Q12.

## The questions

| # | Measures | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | task success — found a saved item while searching | the plain question this survey exists to answer, and the numerator under L1 (Reconnection view rate, Part 6 §6.3) |
| 3 | comprehension — *why* it appeared | panel-sizing.md's own ≥80% target; this is the number that goes in that cell |
| 4 | what was confusing, if anything | free text, only useful when Q3 isn't "clearly" |
| 5 | decision confidence, self-reported (**1–5 Likert scale**) | proxy for L2 doubt-resolution (Part 6) — a number telemetry alone can't produce, because "added to cart" and "resolved and decided not to buy" both look like a terminal state in the logs |
| 6 | good enough to decide, vs. still needs elsewhere | the sharpest test in the set — "helped, but I'd still check elsewhere" is a partial failure of L2 even if Q5 scored high |
| **7** | **"this screen cleared up what I was unsure about" (1–5 Likert scale)** | the direct, quantifiable answer to whether the solution addresses the pain point — sits next to Q5/Q6 rather than folded into either, so it can be reported as its own mean |
| **8** | **matched, exceeded, or fell short of the page-1 feature description (closed choice)** | recovers some of the blind-testing signal given up by "What this prototype offers." Separates "this confirmed the description" from "this told me more than the description did" — only the second is a genuine usability finding rather than the copy on page 1 doing its job |
| 9 | what's still missing, in their own words (free text) | the same test `wishlist-survey.md` Q9 runs on the *idea* of a fix, now run on the *actual* one: if the answer names something the screen already showed, they missed it (a UI problem); if it names something the screen genuinely doesn't say, we built a message |
| **10** | **which named doubt is actually holding them back (closed choice)** | **contradiction check #1.** Same moment as Q9, but as a pick from the corpus's own doubt taxonomy — fit, trust/genuineness, price, return-friction, "decided" — so the panel's self-report can be tallied straight against Part 2's split (fit 46.2% of every named doubt, trust 9.7%) instead of needing free text re-coded first. A tilt toward price here (banned, exogenous) or away from fit would be a real finding for Part 7, not noise |
| 11 | recovery from an unavailable variant | panel-sizing.md's named usability question, direct |
| 12 | what happened when they tapped to buy or save (neutral options, no "correct" one named) | the exact distinction Improvement 3 in the prototype's own README claims to have fixed — asked blind, so a respondent can't infer which outcome the researcher wanted to hear |
| 13 | which action was noticed/tapped first, and why | self-report companion to the §4.4 swapped-fill click telemetry — treat disagreement between this answer and the logged click as a finding, not an error |
| 14 | did sorting the comparison actually help | Compare screen's whole thesis (priority reorders, never hides) |
| 15 | was a pairing suggestion useful | cross-category pairing, kept provisional pending interview evidence (STATUS.md) — this is the panel-scale half of that evidence |
| 16 | overall standalone usefulness (**1–5 Likert scale**), no reminder comparison in the question | the CS1 test, asked blind — see "Blind-testing pass" below |
| 17 | one open ask (free text) | the softest signal on the form; read it, don't build from it alone. Sits directly after Q16 — an unprompted "this is just like a reminder" reaction can surface here on its own, which is a stronger signal than asking them to confirm a comparison they were handed |
| **18** | **what really brings them back to a real, non-demo wishlist (tick-all-that-apply, off the demo)** | **contradiction check #2.** Part 2 §2.4 justifies not building for revisit-intent on the assumption that "revisit is already served by notifications and price-drop alerts." As of the last STATUS.md update, that assumption has zero interview sessions run against it. This question asks the same thing `interview-guide.md` Q6 asks 6 people — but at panel scale (n=300–500). If most respondents tick "I go looking on my own," that directly contradicts the assumption the reconnection prototype was scoped against, and is worth a line in Part 7 either way |

Q19–20 (age, city) are optional and describe the sample only, same convention
as `wishlist-survey.md`.

**On the two contradiction checks (Q10, Q18).** Both are closed-choice by design, not free text — the point is a number you can put next to a corpus number in the same sentence, not another quote to read. Neither replaces the interview track; six laddered conversations will still explain *why* a mismatch exists in a way a tick-box can't. What they buy is a panel-scale sanity check on two assumptions the project is currently resting on interview evidence that, as of writing, does not exist yet.

**There is no upfront task checklist.** An earlier draft opened with "What did
you do just now? (Check all that apply)," meant only to give every later
task-specific question a safe "didn't do this" out instead of Forms
branching. It was dropped: every task-specific question already carries its
own such option directly — Q2's "I didn't have anything saved that matched,"
Q11's "I didn't run into this," Q14/15's "I didn't compare"/"I didn't see
one" — so the checklist was answering a question the rest of the form
already answers one item at a time, asked before the respondent had said
anything else. No branching logic depended on it, so nothing else changes.

## Blind-testing pass

Two questions used to name the "correct" answer inside their own options,
which primes the respondent toward the flattering answer before they've
formed an independent impression of what happened:

- **Q12** used to read "did it feel like a real decision, or just a pop-up?"
  with the options themselves labeled "A real decision, with clear next
  steps" vs. "Just a pop-up message" — the value judgment was baked into the
  choice, not left for the respondent to reach. It now asks what happened
  in neutral, descriptive terms ("It opened next steps or choices for me to
  pick from" / "It showed a brief confirmation and that was it"), so the
  README's own claim can be tested without telling the respondent which
  answer would confirm it.
- **Q16** used to ask "did this feel different from a normal wishlist
  reminder?" with "different" pre-framed as good and "same as a reminder" as
  the failure case — the CS1 test, but handed to the respondent as a leading
  comparison rather than something they'd volunteer themselves. It's now a
  standalone 1–5 usefulness scale with no reminder mentioned in the
  question at all. The free-text question immediately after (Q17) is where
  a genuine "this is just like getting reminded" reaction can still surface
  — unprompted, which is the only way that comparison is worth trusting.

Two of the prose-labeled ordinal questions (confidence, and the new
addressed-the-doubt question) were also converted to true `addScaleItem`
Likert scales (1–5, labeled endpoints only) rather than custom-worded
`addMultipleChoiceItem` options, so they can be reported as a mean rather
than read off as a distribution of radio-button labels.

## Design choices carried over from the wishlist survey, and one dropped

**Time anchors and named subjects, not vague adverbs.** Every question names
"that item" or "that module" outright, for the same reason as before — no
pronoun should depend on a page heading.

**No branching.** `wishlist-survey.md`'s branches exist because an empty
wishlist genuinely has nothing to answer about. Nothing here is like that:
every respondent used the same seeded demo, so instead of branching, any
task-specific question carries a **"didn't do this" / "didn't run into this"**
option and stays on the main path. This is a deliberate simplification —
`update-form-runbook.md` already documents branching as the API's most
fragile corner, and a shorter, unmoderated, at-panel-scale form is exactly
where a silently broken branch would cost the most responses before anyone
noticed.

**No "would this be useful to you?" hypothetical.** Same rule as the wishlist
survey, for the same reason, but it lands differently here: the respondent
just *used* the thing, so every question can be asked in the past tense about
what actually happened, which is a stronger instrument than the discovery
survey had access to.

## What this cannot tell you

- **It is not the 30-day cohort metric.** Nothing here substitutes for W30 or
  for a real production ramp; see `panel-sizing.md` for exactly what a
  300–500 panel can and cannot power.
- **Self-reported confidence (Q5) is not the same as a correct decision.** A
  respondent can feel confident and still be wrong about fit — this form
  measures *felt* doubt-resolution, and G3 (size-recommendation kept-rate, if
  the module ever emits one) is the number that would catch the difference.
- **Q13 is self-report of attention, not a substitute for the click log.**
  Where the two disagree, trust the log and treat the disagreement itself as
  a finding worth a line in Part 7.
- **This is a convenience panel using a seeded demo catalog**, not a
  representative sample or real search traffic — state that once in the deck,
  the same limit `wishlist-survey.md` already states for its own sample.

- **Page 3 asks six questions about a screen the respondent may never have
  reached.** Q2 lets someone answer "No, I didn't notice it" or "I didn't have
  anything saved that matched" — and Q5, Q6, Q7, Q8, Q9 and Q10 then ask them to
  rate that screen anyway. Pages 4 and 5 carry their own "didn't run into this"
  outs; page 3 carries none. Answers from those respondents are invention, not
  missing data. This is a real departure from the principle stated in the `.gs`
  header — that every task-specific question carries its own out — and six of the
  form's most load-bearing questions do not. Handle it in analysis, not by
  patching a form that is already collecting.
- **Q12 has no blind status left.** Page 1's "What this prototype offers" says the
  prototype "gives you real next moves - not just a confirmation message that
  disappears." Q12 then offers "It opened next steps or choices for me to pick
  from" against "It showed a brief confirmation and that was it" — page 1's two
  phrases, in page 1's order, with the first already named as the good one. Q12
  records what respondents recall being told to look for. It is not evidence that
  README Improvement 3 works; the click log is.
- **Q3's comprehension figure inherits the same priming**, more mildly: the feature
  list explains why saved items appear before Q3 asks whether the respondent
  understood why. Treat it as an upper bound, and never quote it against
  `panel-sizing.md`'s ≥80% target without saying so in the same sentence.
- **Q9 is required, so some share of its answers are compliance, not content.**
  "na", "nothing", "-" are respondents who had nothing to add and could not skip.
  Strip them before reading the field as qualitative evidence, and do not count
  them as "no remaining gap."
- **Nothing on this form detects a broken render.** If the prototype failed to load
  or laid out badly on someone's phone, every downstream answer is contaminated and
  no field reveals it. The "didn't do this" options catch task non-completion, not
  technical failure.

## Before you tally anything

Three rules, fixed here in advance so they are not chosen after seeing which way
the numbers fall.

1. **Cross-tab page 3 against Q2, and exclude.** Anyone who answered Q2 with "No, I
   didn't notice it" or "I didn't have anything saved that matched" comes out of
   Q5–Q10 entirely. Report the excluded count beside the n — a reader needs to know
   the confidence mean is over fewer people than filled the form in.
2. **Report counts, not percentages, below n = 30.** "3 of 4", never "75%". One
   respondent moves a percentage by 25 points at n = 4, and a percentage invites a
   comparison the sample cannot support. This applies on the deck as much as here.
3. **Q8 is the only partial recovery of blind signal.** Respondents who pick "I
   didn't read page 1 closely" are the unprimed subgroup, and their Q3 and Q12
   answers are worth more than the rest. At small n this is a note in the text, not
   a subgroup analysis — say that, rather than splitting a handful of responses into
   two groups of two.

## Do not add

- Anything about price, discounts or a hypothetical sale — C‑1 bans it in the
  product, and asking about it here invites an answer nobody can act on.
- A second "would you use this" question. Q2, Q6, Q7 and Q15 already ask the
  same thing behaviourally, which is the only way this project asks it.
- A NPS-style 0–10 recommend score. It answers a marketing question this case
  study isn't grading and would cost a page for no line in Part 6 or Part 7.
