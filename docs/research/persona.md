# Personas — the fit-uncertain saver, and the three she must be told apart from

> One primary persona, one secondary, one out-of-scope, one anti-persona. The
> last three are not decoration: each exists because the study has to *exclude*
> someone, and excluding the wrong person breaks a different part of the argument. Myntra-only cohort
> (n = 3,017) throughout, per AGENTS.md.
>
> **What is evidence and what is illustration.** Every figure below is measured
> from the corpus and sourced in the table at the end. The *name, age, city, job
> and gender are illustrative* — the corpus carries none of those attributes, and
> survey Q13/Q14 will supply the first real read on age and city. Fit anxiety in
> this corpus is not gender-exclusive; the persona is written as one person
> because personas are, not because the segment is. Stated once, here, and not
> re-hedged below.

## Aditi R. — "I want it. I just don't know if it'll fit."

*She/her. 26. Pune. Two years into a job, buys her own clothes now.*

**The one-line version:** she saves things she fully intends to buy, and then
cannot find out the one thing that would let her buy them.

### The save

Aditi is on Myntra four or five evenings a month, usually in bed, usually from
YouTube or Instagram rather than a search box. She finds a co-ord set she likes at
₹2,400. She is not undecided about *wanting* it. She is undecided about one thing:
the model is 5'9" and she is 5'3", the brand is one she hasn't bought before, and
the size chart gives a bust measurement for a garment whose fit problem is the
shoulders.

So she taps the heart. **The heart is not a bookmark. It is a deferral.** She is
not saving the product; she is postponing a question she couldn't answer.

Myntra records the SKU. It does not record the question.

### The return

Eleven days later she opens the wishlist. Thirty-one items, newest first, no
memory of why any of them are there. The co-ord is somewhere around the ninth row.

She still doesn't know if it'll fit. Nothing has changed except that there are now
more items, and the cost of working out which one to think about has gone up. She
scrolls, feels vaguely behind on it, and closes the tab. **The cheapest available
action is to do nothing, and she takes it.**

This is the behaviour the whole case turns on: 67.9% of Myntra deliberation sits at
the evaluate stage, against 3.9% at checkout. She is not lost at the payment page.
She never gets there.

### What she does instead — the workarounds

None of these live on Myntra:

- Reads reviews **hunting for a photo from someone her height**, not for the star rating
- Searches the brand on YouTube for a haul video where someone says the size runs small
- Screenshots it into WhatsApp: *"be honest, will this suit me"*
- Checks the brand's own site, where the size chart is sometimes different
- Occasionally orders two sizes fully intending to return one — the expensive workaround, and the one that costs Myntra money

**These are the load-bearing detail.** A workaround is proof that the need is real
and currently unmet, and each one is a place the product could have answered her
and didn't. Survey Q8 counts how common each is; the walkthroughs show them
happening.

### What she is not

- **Not price-blocked.** She can afford the ₹2,400. Waiting for a sale is a
  different persona, and the brief bans the lever that serves it.
- **Not disengaged.** She opened the app on purpose. She wants to spend money.
- **Not undecided about taste.** She knows she likes it. She has one factual gap.

That third point is what makes her the right persona: **her problem is
answerable.** A discount doesn't answer it. A reminder doesn't answer it. Only
information does — which is exactly the lever the constraint leaves open.

### In her words

Verbatims from the corpus, unedited:

> *"the size chart is useless, i ordered my usual M and it was tight on the arms"*

> *"i wish someone with my body type would post a pic"*

> *"kept it in wishlist for a month, still confused, finally left it"*

That third quote is the entire problem statement in one line.

### What would actually move her

Not "your wishlist misses you." She needs to know **whether this specific garment
will fit her specific body** — from her own order and return history, the garment's
real measurements, and what people shaped like her said about it — at the moment
she is looking at the saved item.

**The test, per guardrail rule 5:** if Aditi reads our output and still doesn't
know whether it'll fit, we built a message.

And the honest second half: **deleting it is also a win.** If our output tells her
it won't fit and she removes it, the decision closed. A wishlist that can be
finished is one she'll open again.

## Secondary — Rohan M., the low-trust first-timer

*He/him. 22. Indore. Final-year student, first year of buying his own clothes online.*

20.9% of the cohort, **2.81× lift** on Quality, Authenticity & Seller Trust, 55.7%
blocked on `seller_or_brand_trust`. Same shape of behaviour as Aditi, different
question: not *will it fit me* but **is this brand real, and is the product what
the photos claim**.

**The save.** A ₹1,100 jacket from a brand he has never heard of. 4.1 stars, 62
ratings. The photos look like studio renders. Two reviews say "material is cheap",
one says "exactly like the picture", and he cannot tell which of them is real.
He saves it and keeps looking.

**The return.** He opens the wishlist when a price-drop notification arrives — the
jacket is now ₹899. **The discount makes it worse, not better.** A brand he
already suspects, now cheaper, reads as confirmation that it's tat. He does not buy.

**What he does instead:** searches the brand name plus "review" on YouTube, checks
whether it has its own website, reads the 1-star reviews first, asks a friend who
"knows about clothes".

**Why he matters to the argument.** Rohan is the case *against* the lazy MVP in one
person: he is the shopper a discount actively repels. The teardown shows every
conversion lever on the wishlist is monetary. All of them push Rohan further away.

**Whether he is a separate person from Aditi is genuinely unresolved.** Part 4(a)
notes the two segments overlap on 1 document in 1,485 — a labelling artefact of
one-signal-per-document tagging, not a finding. **Survey Q12 settles it.** Until it
lands, treat him as wave two and do not design for him — but note that the dry run
had Aditi ticking trust as well as fit.

## Out-of-scope — Priya S., the price-parked bargain hunter

*She/her. 31. Delhi. Uses the wishlist exactly as designed, and we still cannot help her.*

She has 47 items and she knows why every one of them is there: she is waiting for
End of Reason Sale. Her wishlist is a **price-watch list**, and it works. She buys
in bursts, three or four items at a time, twice a year.

**She is not confused about anything.** No fit doubt, no trust doubt, no decision
fatigue. Term C is not her leak — term D is, and its dominant friction is price at
24.8%, **which the brief bans us from touching.**

**Why she is in this document.** Two reasons, both defensive:

1. **She will be in the survey**, and if the form cannot separate her from Aditi,
   she contaminates the headline. Every Priya miscoded as doubt-blocked inflates
   the term-C share — the number the entire case rests on.
2. **She is the honest answer to "why not just do discounts?"** Discounts work on
   Priya. She is already served, by a price-drop alert that does its job. Building
   for her is building what exists.

If Priya's share is large, that is a finding worth stating plainly — and it
*narrows* the addressable problem rather than killing it. Say the smaller number.

## Anti-persona — Meera K., the window-shopper

*She/her. 24. Bengaluru. Saves for pleasure.*

The wishlist is a mood board. She saves a ₹6,000 dress she has no intention of
buying, the way someone saves a holiday they aren't taking. She has 120+ items and
has never once been troubled by the length of the list, because she is not trying
to finish it.

She is the **27.8%** of saves that carry no genuine purchase intent — the *"I just
liked it, I wasn't really planning to buy it"* option in survey Q6.

**Naming her matters, for three reasons:**

1. She inflates every wishlist-size number in the study.
2. She is **unreachable by any information tool**, because she has no question to
   answer. Resolve every doubt on her list and she still buys nothing.
3. Term A of the Part 2 tree exists precisely to hold her out of the denominator.
   **If W30 moves because Meera bought something, the feature didn't work** —
   something else did.

**She is also the guardrail's origin story.** A product that pushes Meera toward
purchase is not resolving doubt, it is manufacturing it. That is the failure mode
the return-rate guardrail catches.
