# Dry run — the form, answered as four personas

> ## ⚠ SYNTHETIC. NOT DATA.
>
> These answers are **invented**, by me, from `persona.md`. They are a pilot of the
> instrument — a check that each question yields something the framework can use —
> and nothing else.
>
> **They must never enter the response sheet, a chart, a percentage, or the deck.**
> If a number in the final submission cannot be traced to a real respondent or to
> the corpus, it does not go in. This file exists to be thrown away once real
> responses land.

Four respondents, chosen for **coverage, not variety**. Each tests something the
instrument has to get right:

| Persona | Tests |
|---|---|
| **Aditi** — fit-uncertain saver | Does the form capture the target problem at all? |
| **Rohan** — low-trust first-timer | Does it separate a *trust* doubt from a *fit* doubt? |
| **Priya** — price-parked | Does it keep a banned-lever shopper **out** of the term-C share? |
| **Meera** — window-shopper | Does it correctly identify a respondent we cannot help? |

### Q9 in full — the only free-text answer, and the MVP's acceptance test

> _"Whether the shoulders will be too wide on me. I'm 5'3" and every model is 5'9",
> so the photos tell me nothing. If someone my height had posted a picture, or if
> it just told me how it fitted people who ordered my usual size, I'd buy it
> tonight."_

Three things are doing work in that sentence, and each one is a build instruction:

| What she says                                      | What it demands                                                  | Buildable from                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| _"the shoulders"_                                  | A **garment-specific** answer, not a generic size recommendation | This SKU's real measurements, not the brand's size chart |
| _"someone my height had posted a picture"_         | Evidence from a **body-similar** shopper                         | Review photos and text, mined for height/build signal    |
| _"how it fitted people who ordered my usual size"_ | Her **own history** as the reference point                       | Her past orders + return reasons against this SKU        |
| _"I'd buy it tonight"_                             | The doubt is the **only** thing between her and the purchase     | —                                                        |

That last clause is the one to keep. She is not price-blocked, not undecided about
taste, not disengaged. Remove the doubt and the sale happens — which is what makes
this problem worth a product rather than a discount.

## Four things this dry run actually surfaced

### 1. Q9 passes the guardrail-5 test — and that is the headline

Her answer names two mechanisms, both buildable, neither a message:

- _"how it fitted people who ordered my usual size"_ → derivable from her own order and return history against this SKU's return-reason data
- _"someone my height had posted a picture"_ → derivable from mining review photos and text for body-similar signal

If she reads that output, **she knows whether it fits.** That clears rule 5's test —
_if the user reads our output and still doesn't know, we built a message_ — and it
does it without a discount, which clears the brief's constraint. The MVP direction
survives contact with the persona.

### 2. Q12 fires exactly as designed, and the result is uncomfortable

Aditi ticked **fit and trust**. Under Part 4(a)'s current framing she is a
fit-uncertain shopper and Rohan is a different person; here they are the same
person with two doubts about different items on one list.

If real responses repeat this, **"lead with fit, trust is the second wave" is the
wrong roadmap** and the MVP has to resolve a _class_ of doubt rather than one
doubt. That is a Part 5 architecture decision, and Q12 is the only instrument that
raises it. Worth noting that a single synthetic respondent proves nothing — but it
proves the _question works_, which is what a pilot is for.

### 3. Q10 says fit is a kill, not a stall

She removed her last item for _"couldn't find out enough to decide"_ — the same
blocker class as Q7. If Q7 and Q10 agree this way at scale, the framing sharpens:
unresolved fit doubt does not park items, it **destroys** them. That is a stronger
sentence for Part 4(c) than anything currently in it, and it comes free from
laying two questions against each other.

### 5. Q7 forced the window-shopper to invent a blocker — **fixed**

Meera told Q6 *"I just liked it, I wasn't really planning to buy it."* Q7 then
demanded the single biggest thing stopping her — and **had no true option.** The
closest were "I don't need it yet" and "I'd forgotten about it until now", neither
of which is what she means. Required question, no branch available mid-page, so
she must pick one.

**Every window-shopper would have injected a spurious blocker into the term-C
distribution** — the one number the entire Part 2 argument rests on. With 27.8% of
saves carrying no genuine intent, that is not a rounding error.

Fixed by adding to Q7: **"Nothing is stopping me — I'm not actually planning to
buy it."** One option, and it makes the headline number honest. This is the
finding that justifies running the pilot at all.

Q8's help text also picked up a clause for the same reason — "if there was nothing
to look up, just tick I didn't try" — since *"did you try to find that out"* is
incoherent for a respondent with no question.

### 4. Two real defects in the form

**Q2 conflates browsing with buying.** "How often do you shop for clothes on
Myntra?" — Aditi _browses_ four or five evenings a month and _buys_ every month or
two. She has to pick one and the two answers mean different things. Every
respondent will resolve that ambiguity differently, which quietly degrades the
segmentation the question was added for. Fixable in four words.

**Q6 and Q7 are single-select, and she had more to say.** Q6: she was also
collecting similar options. Q7: "can't decide between this and something else" is
true for her too — Q11 confirms it. Single-select is still right for both (it
forces a ranking, and multi-select on Q7 would make the "single biggest" framing
meaningless), and Q11 and Q12 now catch the spillover. **Not a defect to fix — a
limit to state**: Q6 and Q7 report the _dominant_ motive and blocker, not the
whole set.

## Recommended change

One edit, from finding 4:

> **Q2:** "How often do you shop for clothes on Myntra?" — APPLIED 20 Aug
> → **"How often do you _buy_ clothes on Myntra?"**

"Buy" is unambiguous, it is the behaviour the segmentation actually wants, and it
keeps the screen intact — "Never" still means not a Myntra customer. Browsing
frequency is not needed by any analysis in the project.

Everything else stands.
