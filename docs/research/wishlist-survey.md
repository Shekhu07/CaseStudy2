# Wishlist survey — 13 questions, ~3 minutes

Generated by `create-wishlist-form.gs`. Paste that into <https://script.google.com>
and run `createWishlistForm`; this file is the rationale behind it.

## What it is for

It is **not** a shorter interview. It does only what a form is uniquely good at:
counting things across many people.

It used to carry an interview opt-in as well. **It no longer does** — the six
walkthrough participants are recruited directly, so the form has no recruiting
job, and with no recruiting job there is no reason to ask anyone for an email.
That makes it genuinely anonymous rather than anonymous-with-an-exception, which
is what the description always claimed and can now stand behind.

It measures four things the 3,922-document corpus **structurally cannot**
(see STATUS.md, "Why almost nothing in here mentions the wishlist"):

| Q | Measures | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | how often the wishlist is revisited | the term the Part 2 tree otherwise assumes |
| 5 | purchases from it in 30 days | the north-star metric itself |
| 6 | why the item was saved | the intent split hidden inside `genuine_intent` 72.2% |
| 7 | what is blocking it | triangulation against the Part 1 theme shares |

Q8 is the fifth: the only free-text box, and the test of whether the MVP resolves
anything.

**Q10 and Q11 were added after reviewing a peer's form** (16 questions, no consent,
no screener, no branching, adverb scales throughout — but it asked two things we
had wrongly deferred). Both are survey-shaped, not interview-shaped: you want a
share across many people, not a probe on six.

| Q | Measures | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | why an item is removed unbought | abandonment — the *other* exit from the Part 2 tree, invisible to Q4/Q5 |
| 11 | what happens with close alternatives | choice overload as behaviour; nothing else here measures it at all |

Q10 reuses Q7's taxonomy, so blockers that **stall** a purchase can be laid against
blockers that **kill** one. Q11's load-bearing option is "end up buying none of
them" — that is the failure a comparison-shaped MVP has to clear, and its size is
currently unknown.

**Q8 and Q12 were added in a stress test of this form against Parts 2 and 4.**
Both close holes the form was assumed to cover and did not.

| Q | Measures | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | whether they tried to resolve the doubt, and where they looked | Part 4(c) — is the doubt *still* unresolved at revisit; Part 4(d) — workarounds, which otherwise has no instrument |
| 12 | which doubts co-occur across one person's whole list | Part 4(a) — whether fit-uncertain and low-trust are one population or two |

Q8's load-bearing option is **"I tried, and still couldn't find out."** A high
share is the root cause stated as behaviour rather than asserted: the doubt is not
laziness, it is unanswerable on the surface where it arises. Part 4(c) says the
corpus "sees the doubt once, never twice" and so cannot show this — without Q8
the form had the same blindness.

Q12 exists because **Q7 cannot do this job.** Q7 is single-select on a single
item, so a shopper who is both fit-uncertain and low-trust picks one and the
overlap is invisible. Part 4(a) concedes the 1-in-1,485 segment overlap is a
labelling artefact of one-signal-per-document tagging; Q12 is the only instrument
in the project that can settle it, and the answer decides whether the MVP serves
one doubt or two.

## The questions

1. Consent *(checkbox)*
2. How often do you **buy** clothes on Myntra? *(4 frequency bands + Never; screens out on Never)*
3. How many items are in your wishlist right now? *(branches out if none)*
4. In the last 30 days, how many times did you open your wishlist?
5. In the last 30 days, did you buy anything from your wishlist?
6. Why did you save that item rather than buy it? *(the 7 motivations + "I honestly don't remember")*
7. What's the single biggest thing stopping you from buying that item? *(12 blockers + "nothing, I'm not planning to buy it" + Other)*
8. Did you try to find that out? Tick everywhere you looked. *(checkbox)*
9. What would you need to know to decide on that item today — buy it, or delete it? *(paragraph)*
10. The last time you removed something without buying it, why? *(8 reasons + never + Other)*
11. When two or three saved items are close alternatives, what usually happens?
12. Across your whole wishlist, which of these are true of at least one item? *(checkbox)*
13. Age
14. Which city?

Q10 and Q11 sit on their own page, after the Q3 branch target — so anyone who
reported an empty wishlist skips both, correctly. They are deliberately *not*
appended to page 4, whose help text scopes its three questions to one specific
item; these two ask about general behaviour.

**Q2 was a plain yes/no and is now a frequency band**, taken from the peer form's
opening question. The same single tap now also describes the sample — "n% shop
monthly or more" is a method-note line a yes/no could never produce, and Q4–Q10
can be cut by shopping intensity at no extra cost.

Two departures from the version it came from. It names **Myntra**, not "online",
because AGENTS.md requires Myntra claims to be quoted Myntra-only. And it uses
time anchors, not *Very often / Often / Sometimes* — one person's "Often" is
another's "Sometimes", and neither can enter a metric tree.

It asks how often they **buy**, not how often they *shop* — a distinction the
persona dry run caught. Someone who browses four evenings a month and buys every
other month cannot answer "how often do you shop" without inventing a rule, and
each respondent invents a different one.

Only **Never** screens out. A few-times-a-year shopper stays in: those are the
longest deferrals in the sample and the most interesting wishlists in it.

Still no list of other apps. Which *other* platforms someone uses feeds none of
the analyses here, so the list would cost a tap and buy nothing.

**There is no interview opt-in.** An earlier version ended with one, plus an email
field on an opt-ins-only page. Both are gone: the six walkthrough participants
come from the researcher's own network, recruited directly. A cold form is a poor
recruiting channel for a call anyway — a stranger has little reason to spend
thirty minutes showing their wishlist to someone they have never met — and
removing it means the form collects nothing identifying at all.

Five pages. One paragraph box. Everything else is a tap. Nothing identifying is asked.

### The page structure, and why it is not shorter

Q2 and Q3 must each be **the last question in their section.** Google Forms
only honours per-answer branching on the final question of a page, and the Apps
Script `setChoices()` call throws *"Invalid data updating form"* if you attach
navigation to a question with anything after it. Adding a question *after* either
one breaks the branch; adding one *before* is fine.

Which merges are and are not available:

| Pages | Merge? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| in-general + About you | ❌ | "About you" is the branch target for an empty wishlist. Merging would drop those respondents onto Q10–Q12, which they cannot answer |
| count + last 30 days | ❌ | Q3 carries the empty-wishlist branch and must stay last on its page |
| last 30 days + last saved item | ✅ **Done**, with mitigation | See below |

**Five pages is the floor.** Both remaining splits carry a branch.

### The 3+4 merge, and what it cost

The objection to merging these was a frame-of-reference change: Q4–Q5 are about
the **whole wishlist**, Q6–Q9 about **one specific item**, and the page break was
silently doing that work. Merged carelessly, "it" flips meaning mid-scroll.

Two mitigations, both applied, and neither optional if this merge is kept:

1. **`addSectionHeaderItem()` between Q5 and Q6.** Google Forms renders it as a
   titled block *inside* a page — so "Now think about the last thing you saved"
   is still a heading, not a line of body text people skim. This is the tool the
   page break was being over-used for.
2. **Every affected question names its subject outright.** "…open **your
   wishlist**", "…buy anything **from your wishlist**", "why did you save **that
   item**", "stopping you from buying **that item**". No pronoun on that page
   depends on a heading any more.

If anyone later edits this page, keep both. Removing the section header re-creates
the ambiguity without any visible sign that it has.

## What was cut, and where it went

Cut for length, all of it better asked in the walkthrough where you can probe:
wishlist age, comparison-set size, occasion dates, the 0–10 still-want score,
out-of-app workarounds, and "what tipped it last time".

Removal behaviour *was* on this list and has been moved back in as Q10 — the call
was wrong. Six interviews give six anecdotes about abandonment; the form gives a
distribution, and abandonment is half the Part 2 tree. Out-of-app checking stays
cut as a standalone question — Q8 now captures it as one option among the places
people look, which is where it belongs. The
playbook's per-item probe script (§3.3) already covers every one of these, and
covers them properly — with the item on screen and a follow-up available.

Deliberate redundancy avoided: occasion-parking and comparison-sets are **options
inside Q6**, so their share is still measured. Only their *magnitude* moved to the
interview.

Two design choices worth keeping if you edit it:

- **Q6 carries "I honestly don't remember."** If that scores high it is not a dead
  option, it is the finding: the wishlist keeps no record of why anything is in it.
- **Q7 reuses the Part 1 blocker taxonomy in plain language**, so the two methods
  can be laid against each other. Disagreement between them is the point.

## When responses land

1. **Q6 distribution** — the intent split Part 1 could not produce. Report
   "deferred decision" and "comparison set" separately.
2. **Q4 → Q5** — the two middle terms of the Part 2 tree, measured not assumed.
3. **Q7 against the theme shares** — same taxonomy, two methods. Explain any
   disagreement rather than smoothing it.
4. **Q7 against Q10** — same taxonomy again, stall versus kill. A blocker that
   appears high in one and low in the other is a finding, not noise.
5. **Q11's "buying none of them" share** — this is the number a comparison MVP
   lives or dies on. If it is small, the fit-and-trust framing carries the deck
   alone.
6. **Q8's "I tried, and still couldn't find out" share** — read this before
   writing Part 4(c). It is the closest thing to a measured root cause the
   project will have.
7. **Q12 cross-tabulated with itself** — how often fit and trust are ticked by the
   same person. This settles Part 4(a) or it does not; either way, say which.

Read Q9 verbatim before coding anything else.

## Limits — state once in the deck, then stop

Convenience sample, self-reported, small n, recruited through my own network and
Reddit. Directional evidence and a recruiting instrument, not a representative
survey; Q5 is self-reported behaviour, not observed conversion.

One further limit worth carrying into Part 2: **Q5 is not W30.** §2.1 defines W30
on a rolling 30 days *from each item's save event*; Q5 asks about the last 30
calendar days regardless of when the item was saved. An item saved six months ago
and bought last week counts in Q5 and not in W30. Q5 is a directional read on the
same behaviour, not the metric. Say all of this plainly, once.

**Q7 carries "Nothing is stopping me — I'm not actually planning to buy it."**
Added after the persona dry run. Without it a window-shopper who answered Q6 with
*"I just liked it"* is still forced to name a blocker, and every one of them
inflates the term-C share — the number the whole Part 2 argument rests on. With
27.8% of saves carrying no genuine intent, that is not a rounding error.

**Q6 and Q7 report the dominant motive and blocker, not the whole set.** Both are
single-select by design — Q7's "single biggest" framing is meaningless otherwise —
and the dry run confirmed a real respondent will have more to say on each. Q11 and
Q12 catch the spillover. State it as a limit; do not "fix" it by going multi-select.

## Do not add

- **Any "would you use this feature?" question.** People lie about the future.
  Every question here asks about the past or present. Concepts get tested in the
  interview, against a stimulus, where you can watch the face.
- Anything proposing a discount or coupon — the brief bans monetary incentives, so
  it invites an answer you cannot act on.
- **Anything you could ask in the interview instead.** That is the rule that got
  this from 20 questions down, and it is the one that keeps completion up.
  Q8, Q10, Q11 and Q12 cleared it: none can be probed usefully at n=6.
